\section{Training: a better loss and an averaged generator}\label{sec:training}

We combine two ideas taken from recent GAN recipes~\cite{huang2024}.

\textbf{A relativistic loss with a penalty (RpGAN + R1).} The baseline's $D$ judges each image alone (``is it real?''),
and becomes overconfident: this is what makes runs collapse (Sec.~\ref{sec:data}). The relativistic loss~\cite{jolicoeur2019}
shows $D$ a real image $x$ and a generated one $\tilde x$ and asks which one is \emph{more} real; since the answer is
relative, $G$ always receives a useful signal. The R1 penalty~\cite{mescheder2018} discourages $D$ from changing its
verdict too abruptly around real images, which helps the two networks settle instead of circling around each other.
With $D(\cdot)$ the raw score of $D$ and $\mathrm{sp}(t)=\log(1+e^t)$:
\begin{align*}
L_D &= \mathrm{sp}\big(D(\tilde x)-D(x)\big) + \tfrac{\gamma}{2}\,\|\nabla_x D(x)\|^2,\\
L_G &= \mathrm{sp}\big(D(x)-D(\tilde x)\big).
\end{align*}
We chose the weight $\gamma$ of the penalty by trying 0.1, 1 and 10 with three seeds each: at 0.1 all runs diverge, 1 is
the best and 10 learns more slowly. The penalty adds about 5\% to the training time.

\textbf{An averaged generator (EMA).} During training, the weights of $G$ keep moving around a good solution. Instead of
keeping the last weights, we keep a running average of them, updated after every step:
$G_\mathrm{EMA}\leftarrow 0.999\,G_\mathrm{EMA}+0.001\,G$ (it mostly reflects the last 1.4 epochs). On CIFAR-10, this
alone improves the FID by 24--29\%~\cite{yazici2019}. It costs nothing: the same run gives both the raw and the averaged
generator, so the two can be compared on exactly the same runs.

\textbf{Result: the gain needs both.} Table~\ref{tab:training} shows that the new loss alone does not change \finc{}, the
EMA alone improves it (36.6 to 30.0), and the two together reach 22.1. The EMA is twice as useful with the new loss as
with the old one ($-15.6$ against $-6.7$; this difference is significant, $p=0.002$).

The per-digit view (Fig.~\ref{fig:training-perdigit}) shows what each piece does. The new loss \emph{rebalances the
digits}: the baseline draws a ``1'' in a quarter of its images and a ``2'' in fewer than 2\%, while RpGAN draws each
digit about 10\% of the time. It also draws hard digits better (the ``2'' goes from 113 to 80) and no run collapsed (0 out
of 12). The global \finc{} hides these gains because the baseline's worst digits are also its rarest. The EMA then
\emph{cleans the strokes of every digit} (Fig.~\ref{fig:training-perdigit}a and Fig.~\ref{fig:samples}b--c), which the
global score does see.

\textbf{Why the two work together (hypothesis).} At the end of training, the new loss keeps $G$ and $D$ near a balance:
$D$ barely tells real from fake, and $G$ oscillates around a good solution. Averaging oscillations lands near their
centre. With the baseline loss, $G$ does not oscillate, it \emph{drifts}: its best checkpoint on validation data is around
epoch 110, after which it gets worse. Averaging the last epoch or so cannot undo a drift.

\takeaway{The new loss makes training stable and balanced across digits; averaging the generator's weights turns this
into a much better score (38.7 to 22.1). Neither is enough on its own.}
