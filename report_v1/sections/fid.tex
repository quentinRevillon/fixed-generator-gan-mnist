\section{Which score should we trust?}\label{sec:fid}

\textbf{How a FID works.} Real and generated images are passed through a pretrained network, and each image is replaced
by the vector of features the network computes (its ``impression'' of the image). Each set of vectors is summarized by its
mean $\mu$ and covariance $\Sigma$, and the FID measures the distance between the two~\cite{heusel2017}:
\[
\mathrm{FID}=\lVert\mu_r-\mu_g\rVert^2+\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(\Sigma_r+\Sigma_g-2(\Sigma_r\Sigma_g)^{1/2}\bigr).
\]
The score therefore depends on \emph{which} network looks at the images. The platform uses Inception-v3, trained on
ImageNet photos (\finc{}). We also built a FID on the features of a small classifier we trained on MNIST (98.7\% accuracy),
called \fcnn{}. It is computed in seconds instead of about 40 minutes, so we could follow it at every epoch, and we expected
it to catch the most common failure of our GAN: since $G$ is never told which digit to draw, it can neglect some digits.
Even comparing real images to real images gives a small non-zero FID (1.5 for \finc{}), which is the best possible score.

\textbf{The two scores can disagree.} Changing the loss (Sec.~\ref{sec:training}) divides \fcnn{} by nine but leaves
\finc{} unchanged. Which one is right? To find out, we damaged \emph{real} images in controlled ways and measured how much
each FID reacts (Fig.~\ref{fig:fid-sensitivity}).

\textbf{What each score sees.} \fcnn{} reacts strongly to the \emph{balance between digits}: removing a single digit from
real images raises it from 2.7 to 40, and giving real images the baseline's digit proportions raises it to 124, close to
the baseline's 162. \finc{} hardly notices either (2.4 and 7.4, for a baseline at 40). Conversely, \finc{} reacts to the
\emph{look of the strokes}: adding noise to a quarter of the images is enough to reach the baseline's score (41), while
\fcnn{} stays low (18). This follows from what each network was trained for~\cite{kynkaanniemi2023}:
our classifier only knows the ten digits (digit identity explains 67\% of its feature variance, 26\% for Inception),
whereas Inception knows textures and shapes. A direct check confirms it: if we resample the baseline's images so that every
digit appears equally often, \fcnn{} drops from 162 to 32, but \finc{} \emph{rises} from 39.9 to 46.2, because the rare digits
that are added are badly drawn.

\textbf{Our choice.} We rank models with \finc{}: it is the platform's score, it varies less between seeds (3--6\% of its
value against 11--25\%) and it is the one used in the literature. \fcnn{} serves as a diagnostic of digit balance. To see
the quality of rare digits, which a global score hides, we also compute a \emph{per-digit FID}: our classifier sorts the
generated images by digit, and each group of 1\,000 images is compared, with \finc{}, to the real images of the same
digit. Fig.~\ref{fig:training-perdigit}a (Sec.~\ref{sec:training}) shows it for our three main models. The baseline's
``2'', for example, scores 113 (real ``2''s: 10), but it is only 1.6\% of the images, so the global FID barely notices it.

\takeaway{The FID on a digit classifier mostly measures whether all digits are drawn equally often; the Inception FID
measures how well they are drawn. We rank with the latter and use the former as a diagnostic.}
